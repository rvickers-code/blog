Agent Based Modelling of the Insurance Industry

Introduction

This article is my review of the 2019 paper “A simulation of the insurance industry: The problem of risk model homogeneity” by Heinrich, Sabuco, and Farmer (Heinrich et al. 2021) (released on arXiv in 2019, published in 2021), and my analysis of their simulation of the insurance industry.

The hypothesis of the paper is that if the insurance industry as a whole uses too few catastrophe risk models, many insurers could make correlated underestimates of risk, and there would be a possibility of a large number of insurers going simultaneously bankrupt. This is quite a plausible idea; they bring up Solvency II:

“Under the European regulatory framework Solvency II, insurance companies are required to use only certified risk models. This has led to a situation in which only a few firms provide risk models, creating a systemic fragility to the errors in these models.”

I don’t think this is right? I haven’t been able to find evidence for the claim that only specific catastrophe risk models can be used in calculating a firm’s Solvency Capital Requirement (SCR). If a firm uses an internal model to calculate its SCR, then that internal model (surely containing a cat risk model) will need to be approved by the regulator, but it seems the main issue is that it is very hard to create a good cat risk model, resulting in a lot of concentration in the cat model space.

I admire the ambition of this paper, because it is vast and probably impossible. To have a simulation of the entire insurance industry that is reliable enough to be able to run prospective policy changes on it, and have the results be anything but very weak evidence would be a great achievement. It would have to be extremely nuanced, and if we were going to trust it, we would also need a track record of successful predictions.

As it is then, this paper represents an interesting starting point, and is also interesting to me personally for the following reasons:

  • I am interested in insurance, and (because it is written in code) a simulation of the insurance industry would have to contain precise statements about how each part of the industry works, so the simulation offers a good learning opportunity.

  • This is a python project of moderate size. I self taught python and have done a medium sized project myself (a reinforcement learning bridge bot, blog post coming soon), so it would be interesting to see how others do it.

So in this article I:

  • explore what happens in the simulation and how realistic it seems
  • explore the approximations/assumptions made by the paper and see if they seem valid

I focus on how the simulation works, not the problem of risk model homogeneity. If someone asked me to give them an analysis of this model of the insurance industry, this is the report I would write.

Objects in the Simulation

Here is a brief summary of the entities in the simulation:

  1. The central InsuranceSimulation object which creates, holds, and prompts all the other objects for action. Each time step InsuranceSimulation.iterate is called, from where every agent has its own iterate method called, from where they take their actions.

  2. Houses (which they call risks) spread across four peril regions (which they call categories). There is no household class in the code as they don’t take any actions, so they are just represented as dictionaries.

  3. Catastrophes: independently, each peril region has a small chance to get hit with a catastrophe each time step. All insurance claims come from catastrophes, so there are no times when just one house makes a claim.

  4. Risk models: these have the exact same catastrophe frequency and severity distributions as was used to generate the catastrophes, except they have an inaccuracy attribute which causes them to underestimate the severity in one peril region by half, and overestimate severity in all the other regions by a factor of two. The objective of the original paper is to study the effect of varying the total number of risk models. If we increase the number of risk models for insurers to choose from (they each only use one), any particular catastrophe is going to catch fewer of them by surprise, so the number of bankruptcies will be less.

  5. Insurers who insure the houses. Since the only insurance claims result from catastrophes, and these affect every house in its region to a similar extent, you could say that each insurer essentially underwrites one massive policy for each peril region; they are hardly diversified at all.

  6. Insurance Contracts: in practice these have no deductible, cover the full value of the house and last for one year.

  7. Reinsurers. Much like for insurers, reinsurance contracts from different insurers but the same peril region are extremely correlated, so reinsurers also basically underwrite one massive policy in each region.

  8. Reinsurance Contracts. These are all aggregate excess-of-loss, have a deductible in \([25\%,30\%]\), have no limit (i.e. cover the full value of the insured houses) last for one year and cover all the insurance policies of an insurer in one region at the time of inception.

In this simulation, every agent takes a variety of actions each time step: the simulation might create new firms, insurers decide which risks to underwrite according to their capital requirement and concentration in a particular region, reinsurers decide which portfolios to underwrite, etc.

My Changes

After fixing a few mistakes, I added plenty of logging, e.g. to record every cashflow for every insurer and reinsurer. They already include logging for the simulation as a whole, but I wanted to look at the experience of each individual (rein-)insurer. Every time series except Figure 1, the left hand side of Figure 3 and Figure 10 come from my logs. The rest are produced by calculations in the preceding code cell.

Their Runs of the Simulation

In the paper, they run each of the 4 risk model configurations 400 times, with each simulation lasting 4000 times steps. Running one simulation for \(1000\) time steps took my computer about 15 minutes, so to replicate their simulation it would take my laptop about

\[\begin{align} \frac{4\times 400\times 4000\text{ time steps}}{66.7\text{ time steps per minute}} &= 96000\text{ minutes} \\ &= 67\text{ days} \end{align}\]

So I can’t try to replicate their results. Instead I did several runs of 5000-1000 time steps while refining what I wanted to measure, and finally did one run of 20000 time steps and analyzed that.

Without doing any statistics, most of the plots below have numbers large enough to make me confident that my conclusions are general. A way my conclusions might not generalize to other runs of the simulation would be that my run somehow got stuck in an unusual region of its state space. But looking at the graph for the number of insurers over time Figure 1, this run seems “well mixed”, i.e. it doesn’t seem to stick anywhere in state space that suggests other runs would be particularly different.

def log_to_time_series(log):
    # I often coded logs as dictionaries to save memory because many cash flows only occur rarely
    # This function converts them all to arrays for plotting
    t0 = log['start_time']
    tf = log['end_time']
    if tf == None: tf = T
    ts = np.arange(t0, tf+1)
    res = {'ts':ts}
    for k in log.keys():
        d = log[k]
        if k != 'reinrisks_created':
            if type(d) == list:
                res[k] = np.array(d)
            elif type(d) == dict:
                ys = np.zeros(tf-t0+1)
                for t in d.keys():
                    ys[t-t0] = d[t]
                res[k] = ys
        elif k == 'reinrisks_created':
            ys = np.zeros(tf-t0+1)
            for t in d.keys():
                ys[t-t0] = len(d[t])
            res[k] = ys
    return res

Some fixes could be:

  1. Increase the deductible on reinsurance contracts, therefore making them cheaper and more likely to get underwritten
  2. Allow multiple reinsurers to take different layers of each reinsurance contract so the increase in capital requirement from each policy is smaller
  3. Currently, each reinsurance request gets seen by exactly 1 reinsurer, who is chosen at random. This could be changed to only send reinsurance requests to reinsurers who could actually underwrite them (determined using a quick rule of thumb, like “is the reinsurer’s cash more than 5 times the maximum loss on the reinsurance contract?”). This would also help large reinsurers because large requests don’t get sent to tiny reinsurers.

Given the outlier with a loss ratio of 14, other similar runs could probably show a profitable reinsurance industry. Either way, this extreme variability probably means no one would want to set up a reinsurance business here.

The points I made above about reducing the number of firms who immediately exit would in my opinion also make reinsurers’ experiences more stable, but the fundamental point is that there are only 4 risk regions, so reinsurers are very un-diversified.

Reinsurers are often global and diversify across many different lines of business, so we would need a much bigger simulation with many different types of uncorrelated claims to reproduce a realistic reinsurance sector.

Bankruptcy Risk

One thing I will explore in this article is whether insurers hold enough capital to cover the \(99.5^{\text{th}}\)-percentile of losses. The simplest way to model this is to imagine that each insurer independently has a probability \(p\) of going bankrupt each time step. Then if there are \(O_t\) firms operational at the start of a time step, the number of bankruptcies \(B_t\) will be distributed as \(B_t\sim\text{Binom}(O_t,p)\).

Catastrophes

Catastrophes damage all houses in the region they hit. There are no single-house claims; the only claims events are catastrophes.

Catastrophes times for each region are independent Poisson processes with rate \(\lambda = 0.03\text{ yr}^{-1} = (33.3\text{ yr})^{-1}\). So on average there are 100 time steps between catastrophes (\(33.3\text{ years}\cdot 12\text{ time steps per year}/4\text{ regions}\)).

In this model, catastrophe size \(C\) is interpreted as the expected fraction of house value that is damaged. Thus they upper bound the catastrophe size distribution at \(1\). They also lower bound it at \(0.25\), because they say an event any smaller isn’t really a catastrophe. The probability densities for catastrophe size \(C\) and the actual fraction of house value that is damaged by the catastrophe \(D\) are:

\[\begin{align} f_C(c) = \begin{cases} \frac{N}{c^3} & 0.25\leq c\leq 1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \\ D|C \sim \text{Beta}(1,\frac{1}{C}-1) \end{align}\]

Insurance

Households (“Risks”)

The InsuranceSimulation object has as an attribute a list of all dictionaries, one for each house. These dictionaries are created by the following line in the InsuranceSimulation constructor:

self.risks = [{"risk_factor": rrisk_factors[i], 
               "value": rvalues[i], 
               "category": rcategories[i], 
               "owner": self} 
               for i in range(self.simulation_parameters["no_risks"])]

where:

  • risk_factor is the probability that, given a catastrophe hits this house’s region, the house is affected. These seem to be disabled by default (i.e. all \(=1\)), and some of the VaR calculations implicitly assume the risk factors are all \(1\) (more on this in the VaR sections).

  • value is the house’s value, which are all \(1000\).

  • category is the peril region: 0, 1, 2 or 3.

  • owner is the person who buys insurance. In this paper, all the policyholders are taken to part of an economy that isn’t modelled; premiums are paid from a giant pool of money which is also where insurers pay dividends into. The owner is self here because the InsuranceSimulation object holds this money pool (simulation.money_supply). When insurers ask for reinsurance they will create “reinrisks”, whose owner will be the insurer themselves.

Insurance Contracts and Pricing

If an insurer underwrites the house, this dictionary is moved to the insurer, wrapped in a InsuranceContract object, and stored in insurer.underwritten_risks.

By default there are \(20\,000\) houses spread over \(4\) risk regions (called categories in the code). There are parts of the code that let them have a value, \(v\), and for insurance policies to have a limit \(L\) (they call this an excess in the paper) and a deductible, \(Q\).

As mentioned above all the house values are hard-coded to 1000, and it seems that they always use \(L=v\) and \(Q=0\) (these are the defaults, and when an insurancecontract object is created it there is no way to not use the default).

Without deductibles or limits, each claim is just \(vD\), where \(D\) is the beta-distributed with mean \(C\), the catastrophe severity. If the number of claims from a given contract is \(N\), the total claim \(S\) (ignoring time value of money) from an insurance contract is

\[ S = \sum_{i=1}^NX_i = \sum_{i=1}^NvD_i = v\sum_{i=1}^ND(C_i) \]

where \(i\) runs over the catastrophes that occur during the policy duration \(T\), and \(X_i\) is the corresponding claim (risk factors are all \(1\) so every catastrophe leads to a claim). Because the time between catastrophes is exponentially distributed with rate \(\lambda\), \(N \sim \text{Poi}(\lambda T)\). So \(S\) has a compound Poisson distribution, and the expectation of total claims from a policy is:

\[ E[S] = vE[N]E_C[E_{D}[D|C]] = v(\lambda T)E[C] \] Where \(v=1000\), \(\lambda = 3/100 \text{ year}^{-1}\), \(T = 1 \text{ year}\) and \(E[C] = 0.4\).

Reinsurance

Reinsurance Contracts

For this simulation, all reinsurance policies are aggregate excess of loss, cover all of the insurer’s the policies that then exist in one region, have a deductible chosen uniformly at random from \(25\%\) to \(30\%\) of the total insured value, and have no limit. To be precise, if \(n_C\) catastrophes hit the reinsured region, and the reinsurance policy covers \(n_H\) houses, then the total claim the reinsurer will have to pay is:

\[ S = \max(0,\sum_{i=1}^{n_C}\sum_{j=1}^{n_H}(vD_{ij}) - Q) \]

Where \(v\) is the house value, \(D_{ij}\) is the damage inflicted by the \(i\)th catastrophe on the \(j\)th house and \(Q\) is the deductible.

Because the catastrophe size \(C\) is lower bounded by \(0.25\), it is possible to have a deductible that basically guarantees a reinsurance claim on any catastrophe. Reinsurance Pricing

To price reinsurance, typically we would compute the expected value of claims (\(S\) in the previous subsection) and then mark it up slightly. From what I can work out, reinsurance premiums here are always 30% of the premiums the insurer receives for the same portfolio:

\[ \text{Reinsurance Premium} = 0.3 \cdot (vn_H \cdot \lambda T\cdot E[C]\cdot 1.15) \cdot \text{RMF} \]

Where:

  • \(0.3\) is the “reinsurance premium share”. They don’t explain why they use 30% and this is definitely aggregate XOL reinsurance, not proportional reinsurance (all reinsurance contracts here have ReinsuranceContract.insurancetype == "excess-of-loss")
  • \(v\) is the value of a single house
  • \(n_H\) is the number of covered houses
  • \(\lambda TE[C]\) is the fair premium for an insurance contract covering one house
  • 1.15 is a markup for profit
    • \(vn_H \cdot \lambda T \cdot E[C] \cdot 1.15\) is the premium the insurer receives for the underlying portfolio, not including the factor for how well capitalized the insurance sector is
  • \(\text{RMF}\) is the Reinsurance Markup Factor. It is between 0.7 and 1.35 and decreases with the total capital in the reinsurance sector

    house_value = 1
    
    deductible = deductible_frac*n_houses*house_value

    mu = 100/3*12 # 33.3 year average cat separation 
    # Discretizing a Poisson process for t << mu. P(no event) = exp(-t/mu), P(1 event) = 1-exp(-t/mu)
    p = 1-np.exp(-1/mu)
    # Sample catastrophe times
    C_times = scipy.stats.binom(1, p).rvs(n_sim*12)
    # Generate n years of damages for each region:
    C_damages = sim.damage_distribution.rvs(n_sim*12)
    # Multiply to only use damages when there was a cat
    Cs = C_times * C_damages
    # 1st index is realisations, 2nd is months
    Cs = np.reshape(Cs, (n_sim, 12))

    claim_realisations = np.zeros(n_sim)
    for year in range(n_sim):
        Cats = Cs[year, :]
        if sum(Cats) > 0:
            Cats = Cats[Cats > 0]
            Ds = scipy.stats.beta(1, 1/Cats-1).rvs((n_houses, len(Cats)))

    claim_realisations = np.fmax(0, claim_realisations-deductible)
    prem = np.mean(claim_realisations)
    return prem

Estimating Value at Risk

Every time an insurer decides whether to write a new policy, it needs to compute whether it will still have enough cash to cover its capital requirement. At worst this means recalculating the \(99.5^{\text{th}}\)-percentile of losses for the insurer’s entire portfolio with this new potential policy added. Since there are typically about \(15000\) underwritten risks at any time (see the left panel of Figure 3), and policies last for 12 months, the simulation would have to evaluate a VaR roughly \(15000/12 \approx 1200\) times per time step.

Therefore, to cut this down to once per insurer per time step, they use several approximations to calculate the SCR:

  1. They assume that at most one region will have a catastrophe each year. (This is problematic given the units error for catastrophe frequency, but would otherwise be a good approximation.)

  2. Companies hold enough capital to cover one catastrophe in their most exposed region. This is more demanding than holding the \(99.5^{\text{th}}\)-percentile of losses, because the latter isn’t conditional on a catastrophe occurring, but is closer to what this industry in these risk regions would do if it existed

  3. Given the catastrophe intensity \(C\), they approximate each house as contributing its mean claim at that catastrophe size to the value at risk, multiplied by its risk factor (each house \(i\) contributes \(r_iE[\text{Claim}|C]\))

Taken together, these approximations also allow them to avoid any Monte Carlo computations, which hugely speeds up the simulation.

  1. expected_profit is None because it is not implemented. Insurers do not consider whether taking a new policy will be profitable. This is ok here because the pricing is set up so that profit is likely

  4. var_per_risk_per_categ is the increase in VaR for that region that comes from underwriting one additional house
  5. excess_capital is the amount by which the insurer’s current cash exceeds its SCR

VaR approximation 1: only 1 region has a catastrophe

With the intended catastrophe frequency \(\lambda=0.03\text{ yr}^{-1}\), they compute in the paper that the probability of 2 or more regions having a catastrophe in a given year is \(1-(e^{-4\lambda} + 4(1-e^{-\lambda})e^{-3\lambda})=0.00504\).

This usefully means that the catastrophe regions are approximately mutually exclusive, so the upper quantiles of claims {}{} for the whole portfolio is just the maximum of the VaR’s for each region:

\[ \text{Solvency Capital Requirement} = \max(\text{VaR}_1,\text{VaR}_2,\text{VaR}_3,\text{VaR}_4) \]

So provided the \(\text{VaR}\) of each region is less than the insurers current cash, it has enough cash to cover its \(\text{SCR}\).

VaR approximation 2: Estimate Losses from Exactly 1 Catastrophe

Holding capital to cover the next catastrophe is more stringent than holding the \(99.5^{\text{th}}\)-percentile of Losses, because the latter isn’t conditional on a catastrophe occurring.

To unpack this, consider random variables \(Y\), \(X_0\), \(X_1\), \(X_2\), …, where \(Y\) is equal to \(X_i\) with probability \(p_i\). Denote the CDFs \(F_Y\) and \(F_i\) respectively. Then

\[\begin{align} F_Y(y)=P(Y\leq y) &= P(Y=X_0 \text{ and } X_0\leq y) + P(Y=X_1 \text{ and } X_1\leq y) + ... \\ &= p_0F_0(y)+p_1F_1(y)+p_2F_2(y)+...\\ &= \sum_{i}p_iF_i(y) \end{align}\]

This applies to our simulation if we let \(Y\) be the yearly claims from a region and \(X_i\) be the claims if \(i\) catastrophes occur (so for example \(X_0=0\) always). Then the \(p_i\) are just the Poisson distribution e.g. \(p_0=e^{-\lambda}=0.970\). So if we want the \(99.5^{\text{th}}\)-percentile of yearly claims, \(q_{0.995}\), we get:

\[\begin{align} 0.995 = F_Y(q_{0.995}) &= p_0F_0(q_{0.995})+p_1F_1(q_{0.995})+... \\ &= p_0\times 1+\sum_{i\geq 1}p_iF_i(q_{0.995})\\ &= 0.970+\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{\lambda^i}{i!}e^{-\lambda}F_i(q_{0.995}) \end{align}\]

If we further assume (as they do) that at most \(1\) catastrophe occurs we get:

\[\begin{align} 0.995 &\approx 0.970+(1-0.970)F_1(q_{0.995}) \\ F_1(q_{0.995}) &\approx (0.995-0.970)/0.0296 \\ &\approx 0.83 \end{align}\]

So the \(99.5^{\text{th}}\)-percentile of claims from one year and region corresponds to the \(83^{\text{rd}}\)-percentile of damages from one catastrophe.

Going the other way: if \(d_{0.995}^{(1)}\) is the \(99.5^{\text{th}}\)-percentile of damages from one catastrophe, what quantile of the yearly losses is \(d_{0.995}^{(1)}\)?

\[\begin{align} F_Y(d_{0.995}^{(1)}) &= p_0F_0(d_{0.995}^{(1)})+p_1F_1(d_{0.995}^{(1)})+p_2F_2(d_{0.995}^{(1)})... \\ &\geq p_0\times 1+p_1\times 0.995 \\ &\geq 0.99941 \\ F_Y(d_{0.995}^{(1)}) &= p_0F_0(d_{0.995}^{(1)})+p_1\times 0.995+p_2F_2(d_{0.995}^{(1)})... \\ &\leq p_0\times 1+p_1\times 0.995 + p_2 \times 0.995 + ... \\ &\leq p_0\times 1+(1-p_0)\times 0.995 \\ &\leq 0.99985 \\ \end{align}\]

(The second group of inequalities follow from the fact that the upper quantiles of damage from one catastrophe are quite low quantiles of damages from more than one catastrophe, so \(F_2(d_{0.995}^{(1)}) < F_1(d_{0.995}^{(1)})=0.995\)).

So reserving for the \(99.5^{\text{th}}\)-percentile of catastrophes is equivalent to reserving for something in the \(99.941^{\text{th}}\) to \(99.985^{\text{th}}\)-percentile range of yearly losses. So for infrequent catastrophes, reserving for one catastrophe is a lot more stringent than reserving for yearly losses.

I think this approximation is good practise though. Insurers are there to cover claims, so they should have enough money to cover a bad claims event. We can imagine that if catastrophes were rare enough, the \(99.5^{\text{th}}\)-percentile of losses could be absolutely tiny compared to actual losses in a catastrophe, meaning insurers would never actually be able to cover losses.

VaR approximation 3: Each house contributes its expected claim, given the catastrophe size

Because of approximation 1, they can calculate VaRs in the 4 peril regions separately and take the maximum.

Focusing on one region then, we would ideally compute 0.995 quantile of total claims, \(S\):

\[ S = \sum_{i=1}^NvD_i = v\sum_{i=1}^ND(C_i) \]

Where, as above, \(D|C \sim \text{Beta}(1,C^{-1}-1)\) so \(E[D|C]=C\).

What approximation 3 means is that they assume the 0.995 quantile of claims comes from a 0.995 quantile catastrophe (\(C=0.9645\)), and they approximate each \(D_i\) in such a catastrophe by \(D_i \approx E[D|C]=C=0.9645\). This simplifies \(S\) to:

\[ S = vN\cdot 0.9645 \] This then gets multiplied by the risk model inaccuracy for this region (0.5 or 2), and the safety margin of 2.

This means that when deciding to underwrite a new policy, the new VaR is just the old one plus the contribution from this new house.

In order, the quantities the above code achieves is:

RiskModel.compute_expectation:

For each region \(c\) (called category in the code), loop over the policies for house in that region, and compute:

\[ \text{AvgExposure} = \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{i=1}^{N_c}(v_i-Q_i) \] Where \(i\) labels the policy in category \(c\), \(v_i\) is the underwritten house’s value (here it is always 1000), \(Q_i\) is its deductible (which is always \(0\)), \(N_c\) is the number of houses with policies with this insurer in region \(c\). As mentioned in the Houses and Contracts{proper name}{} section, all the house values are the same and the deductible are all zero so \(\text{AvgExposure}\) will just be \(1000\). Then compute:

\[ \text{AvgRiskFactor} = I_c \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{i=1}^{N_c}r_i \] where \(r_i\) is its risk factor and \(t_i\) is its remaining run-time, and \(I_c\) is the risk model inaccuracy for region \(c\).

It seems a bit strange to include the inaccuracy in the average risk factor, but it does propagate through the calculations as intended.

Note that under VaR approximation 2, we ignore the remaining run-time on policies and instead imagine one catastrophe hitting all the houses in region \(c\).

RiskModel.evaulate_proportional:

Having computed the above summaries of the policies in each region, compute the following:

\[ q = \text{0.995 quantile of catastrophe severity distribution, accounting for reinsurance} \] This is the correct quantity to compute because of approximation 3; strictly we would want the 0.995 quantile of \[ S = \sum_{i=1}^NvD_i = v\sum_{i=1}^ND(C_i) \] But they approximate \(D(C) = E[D|C] = C\), so the 0.995 quantile of \(S\) is \(vNq\). With reinsurance, this approximation becomes exact because reinsurance deductibles are so low that long before a 0.995 quantile storm, insurers will meet their retention limit.

Then put it all together:

\[ \text{VarPerRisk(c)} = q(\text{AvgRiskFactor})(\text{AvgExposure})(\text{SafetyMargin}) \\ \]

Values-at-Risk only add when the random variables being added are perfectly correlated, so the idea of a VaR per policy only makes sense when the claims are perfectly correlated, i.e. all the risk factors are 1 and all the damages are the same.

I don’t agree with how they include risk factors in this calculation when they aren’t all one.

If we let \(I_i\) be an indicator variable which is 1 when house \(i\) gets damaged, and \(N\) be the number of houses that will experience a claim from this catastrophe, then \(I_i \sim Bin(1,r_i)\) and \(N = \sum_i I_i\).

To get the 0.995 quantile of claims, we should use a high quantile of \(N\), not its mean (as they do, through \(\text{AvgRiskFactor}\)).The SCR must take into account the possibility that a very unluckily large fraction of the houses get affected by the catastrophe.

The 0.995 quantile of \(N\) may be pessimistic because there is likely a bit of diversification between lots of houses getting hit (\(N\) large) and lots of houses getting damaged a lot (the \(\{D_i|i=1,...,n\}\) are large). But at this level of precision it’s probably fine.

From a CLT approximation the 0.995 quantile of \(N\) is \(\sum_i r_i + z_{0.005}\sqrt{\sum_i r_i(1-r_i)})\).

This may be why by default in the code risk factors are all set to 1 (simulation_parameters["risk_factors_present"] == False), so I hope for the runs they did in the paper they had risk factors disabled.

The safety margin is 2, so all in all we have \(\text{VarPerRisk(c)} = qI_c\times 1000 \times 2\)

Because VaR’s for houses within a region add, and the VaR for the whole portfolio is just the maximum of the VaR’s for each region, provided the number of policies in every region \(c\), \(N_c\) satisfies \(N_c \cdot \text{VaRPerRisk(c)} \leq \text{Cash}\) they are solvent. Hence they compute:

\[ \text{AcceptableByCategory(c)} = \lfloor\frac{\text{Cash}}{\text{VarPerRisk(c)}}\rfloor \\ \] So we can underwrite this many more policies in \(c\): \[ \text{RemainingAcceptable} = \text{AcceptableByCategory}(c) - N_c \] and still be solvent.

They then compute a measure of the firms capital utilisation, the difference between the firm’s cash and the \(\text{VaR}\) of each region:

\[ \text{CashLeftByCateg}(c) = \text{Cash}-N_c\cdot\text{VarPerRisk}(c) \]

This is large for regions \(c\) that have hardly any policies or are low risk.

In my opinion they then do quite a strange thing:

\[ \text{Remaining Acceptable(c)} \leftarrow \text{Remaining Acceptable(c)}\times\left(\frac{\text{Cash left(c)}}{\max(\text{Cash left(c)})}\right)^5 \]

The effect of this is greatly reduce the number of new policies the insurer will be willing to write in regions other than its least concentrated region (i.e. where \(\text{Cash left(c)}\ll \max(\text{Cash left(c)})\), which makes sense I guess, but in my opinion it make more sense to move this functionality into the part of the code that decides whether to underwrite new policies. Also, the fifth power isn’t explained.

As they seem to do in their main runs, I assume:

  1. Insurance policies have 0 deductible and cover the full value of the house (these are the defaults, and when an InsuranceContract object is created it seems there is no way to not use the default)
  2. Risk factors are all 1 (if there is a catastrophe in a region, it affects all the houses there)
  3. Policies maturing and policies that will be written in the coming 12 months cancel out, i.e. every current policy will enter the calculation as if it lasts the coming 12 months.
  4. Every house has the same value. For simplicity, I will compute VaRs in units of house value

Here, reinsurance always covers every house underwritten by the insurer in a particular region.

The algorithm I use for simulating a year claims for an insurer is:

  1. Sample a sequence of catastrophe times for each risk region. Since cat times come from a Poisson process with a large mean time between events \(\mu=33.3\text{ years}\), this gets discretised to a Bernoulli process with probability \(p=1-e^{-1/\mu}\) of an event per time step
  2. For each catastrophe, sample a catastrophe size \(C\) from the truncated power law described in the Catastrophe Damages section
  3. For each catastrophe and for each region and for each house \(i\), sample the damage inflicted \(D_i|C \sim \text{Beta}(1,1/C-1)\)
  4. For each catastrophe and for each region compute the aggregate claim: \(v\sum{D_i}\) if the region isn’t reinsured, and \(min(v\sum{D_i}, Q\times(\text{Total value of reinsured houses})\) if it is
  5. Sum over catastrophes and regions

I then repeat this \(100\,000\) times and take the 0.995 quantile to compute the SCR:

Extending the Simulation

There are many potential ways to make the simulation better reflect reality. Of course, these would greatly slow down the simulation, because they would require changing the VaR calculations to a Monte Carlo estimate. I order these in my estimation of descending importance:

  1. Make the different risk models insurers use agree on expected values of risks, but disagree in the tails, or on correlations between risks. This is more plausibly how real risk models disagree, because these are difficult to gauge, and large differences in expected values of claims would be quickly noticed and investigated. Perhaps one risk model could assume all risk factors are the same, but another uses a beta-binomial model for predicting the number of claims with the same mean but a larger variance.

  2. Add more lines of business to allow diversification: because claims in each region are so correlated, each (re)insurer essentially underwrites four massive policies. The pricing is set up so that this is actually profitable for insurers, but I don’t think any real people would want to set up an insurer in this kind of market as it is too volatile.

  3. Allow for competition: currently all insurers offer the same price. Competition on price would reduce margins and perhaps make the “few risk models leads to systemic risk” point clearer. This might lead to insurance being dominated by only a few massive firms because highly capitalised firms can offer the lowest price; perhaps there would need to be some other effect that can lead to smaller insurers being profitable also e.g. local expertise.

  4. Reinsurers taking only a layer of risk; allows smaller reinsurers to do something, not just exit immediately

Predicting what the next changes should be gets very difficult now because the above changes would already significantly change the simulation.

Conclusion

Insurance is often about trying to plan for things do not have past analogues. This might make it impossible for simulations of this kind to be useful, because they can only contain what we thought to put in.

There are zillions of details that could be built into a simulation to make it more accurate. Most are not necessary for something to be reasonably called a simulation of the insurance industry, but how many are necessary to be able to trust predictions about the effects of policy change? We’d have to know how behaviour would change in response to the policy, which seems very hard.

In Appendix B.1 of the paper they write “We generally set \(\lambda = 3/100\), that is, a catastrophe should occur on average every 33 years”. Indeed, in the isleconfig.py file, which contains all the default values for parameters, they set simulation_parameters["event_time_mean_separation"] = 100/3.

This shows that the rate is around 0.03 per time step - the issue is that everywhere else in the simulation the unit of time is months.

For example, also in isleconfig.py they set simulation_parameters["mean_contract_runtime"] = 12 and simulation_parameters["default_contract_payment_period"] = 3. Clearly these are to be interpreted as months, but then the mean event separation is \(33.3 \text{ months} \approx 2.8 \text{ yr}\) which is very frequent indeed.

However, the aim of the paper is to study the effect of risk model homogeneity and the catastrophe rate issue might affect different simulation runs the same way, so perhaps this doesn’t change the conclusion. But it could though, so this mistake reduces the strength of the evidence the paper presents somewhat.

Unless stated otherwise, I will analyse the paper as if the catastrophe rate was \(0.03\text{ yr}^{-1}\).

VaR approx 1: With a frequency of \(\lambda=0.03\text{ month}^{-1}\), the probability of 2 or more regions having a catastrophe in a given year is (by the same calculation) \(0.352\), which is problematic because after the 1st catastrophe there often won’t be enough time for premiums to rebuild the insurer’s capital (even though premiums correctly reflect the catastrophe frequency {}{{}} is this true?). This means there will be a lot more bankruptcies than intended.

VaR approx 2: For catastrophes on average every \(33.3\) months, the same calculation gives the \(99.5^{\text{th}}\)-percentile of catastrophes is equivalent to reserving for the \((97.3\pm2.5)^{\text{th}}\)-percentile of yearly losses, which is not as bad as I would have predicted. However, then we would often have cats in multiple regions each year, so the SCR computed this way would be even more underestimated.
